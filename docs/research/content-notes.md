# GlowGuide educational content research

Research notes for the educational pages of GlowGuide, a UK-facing, Korean-skincare-led site for complete beginners of all ages. Everything below is paraphrased in plain English for a nervous first-timer. Each section ends with the sources that were actually used for it. Sources were weighted in this order: NHS, British Association of Dermatologists (BAD, via skinhealthinfo.org.uk), NICE, American Academy of Dermatology (AAD), British Skin Foundation, Cancer Research UK, then clinic and K-beauty retailer or brand explainers for routine specifics. Where a point comes from brand or retailer content, that is flagged, because those pages also sell products.

A note on the owner-supplied Tira Beauty article (Dr Monica Jacob): it was read in full and used as one source among many. Its ingredient claims are treated as beauty-industry guidance, and anything medical was checked against NHS, BAD, NICE or AAD material.

Terms used throughout, explained once:
- Barrier: the outermost layer of skin, made of dead cells and fats, that keeps water in and irritants out.
- Active: an ingredient that makes a measurable change to skin (acids, retinoids, vitamin C and so on), as opposed to something that mainly hydrates or softens.
- Humectant: an ingredient that pulls water into the skin (glycerin, hyaluronic acid).
- Emollient: an ingredient that softens and smooths skin by filling gaps between cells (squalane, many oils).
- Occlusive: an ingredient that sits on top and slows water escaping (petrolatum, some silicones).
- Non-comedogenic: made so it is less likely to block pores.
- Broad-spectrum: a sunscreen that protects against both UVB (burning rays) and UVA (ageing and pigmentation rays).

## 1. The five-step routine (cleanse, tone, treat, moisturise, protect)

### What each step does

**Cleanse.** Washing takes off oil, sweat, dust, pollution, leftover sunscreen and makeup so the rest of the routine has clean skin to work on. The NHS suggests washing twice a day with a gentle cleanser, and NICE (the body that writes UK clinical guidance) specifically recommends a non-alkaline cleanser for acne-prone skin, meaning one that is pH-neutral or slightly acidic rather than soap-like. The AAD describes the technique: wet the face with lukewarm water, work a mild cleanser in with fingertips in small circles, rinse well, then pat dry with a clean towel. Scrubbing or rough flannels are out, because rubbing irritates skin and makes most problems worse.

**Tone.** In Korean skincare a toner is a thin, watery layer applied straight after cleansing. Its job is to add the first bit of water back, make skin feel comfortable again, and prepare it for anything that comes next. This is different from the old-fashioned alcohol toners many UK adults remember, which were designed to strip oil. K-beauty toners are mainly hydrating (Tira, Misshaus). Press a few drops in with clean hands or sweep over with a cotton pad.

**Treat.** This is where a serum, essence or ampoule goes. An essence is a light, watery hydrating layer; a serum is more concentrated and aimed at one concern, such as redness, dark marks, dryness or spots (Misshaus). Beginners should pick one treatment product for their main concern and leave it at that (Tira).

**Moisturise.** A moisturiser seals in the water from the previous layers and supports the barrier. Choose the texture for your skin: a gel or light lotion for oily skin, a cream for dry skin. Moisturiser absorbs best when applied to slightly damp skin, ideally within a few minutes of washing (Cleveland Clinic, AAD).

**Protect.** Sunscreen is the last step every morning: broad-spectrum, SPF 30 or higher, applied generously (NHS, Tira). Section 7 covers UK labelling and how much to use.

### Morning versus evening

- **Morning (AM):** cleanse (a gentle cleanser, or just lukewarm water if skin is very dry), tone, treat with something that suits daytime (a hydrating serum, or vitamin C once you are ready for it), moisturise, then sunscreen. Sunscreen is only a morning step.
- **Evening (PM):** cleanse (double cleanse if you wore sunscreen or makeup, see section 2), tone, treat with anything that belongs at night (retinoids and exfoliating acids are evening ingredients because they make skin more sun-sensitive), moisturise. No sunscreen at night. A facial oil, if you use one, goes last.
- The AAD recommends retinoids at night only, and both Paula's Choice and John Bell & Croyden suggest vitamin C in the morning and retinoids at night as the simplest way to keep them apart.

### How long to wait between steps

Less than most people think. A dermatologist quoted by Soko Glam says a few seconds of patting a product in is enough before the next layer; the idea of waiting many minutes for each layer to "sink in" is a myth. A practical rule from Liz Earle (a UK brand) is to wait until the previous layer feels touch-dry, meaning not slippery or wet: roughly 20 to 40 seconds for watery toners and serums, up to a minute for creams. The exceptions:
- Rub sunscreen in and give it about a minute before anything goes on top, because other products can dilute it (Soko Glam). If you wear makeup, give an SPF moisturiser two to five minutes to settle first (Liz Earle).
- Let a prescription acne treatment absorb before adding anything else (Soko Glam).
- If an exfoliating acid feels sharp on your skin, waiting longer before moisturiser can help.
- If products start to ball up or peel off, you are using too much or not waiting long enough for the previous layer to dry.

### How much product to use

Rough household measures (brand-specific instructions win if they differ):
- Cleanser: about a 10p-coin-sized blob of gel or cream, or one to two pumps of foam.
- Toner: a few drops or two to three pumps, enough to leave skin feeling damp.
- Serum: two to three drops for the face and neck. More adds no benefit and leaves tackiness (The INKEY List, for hyaluronic acid).
- Retinoid or prescription cream: a pea-sized amount for the whole face, applied as a thin film (UK adapalene leaflet, AAD).
- Moisturiser: a 5p to 10p coin for the face, more for the neck.
- Sunscreen: a bit more than half a teaspoon for face, neck and ears together (BAD fact sheet), or the "two finger lengths" of sunscreen for the face alone (AAD). This looks like far too much the first time. It is the right amount.

### Order of layering: thin to thick

Apply products from the thinnest, most watery texture to the thickest. Water-light layers go on first so they are not blocked by heavier ones, and the heaviest layer (moisturiser in the evening, sunscreen in the morning) goes last (Misshaus, Liz Earle, CeraVe). If two products have a similar texture, put the one with the active ingredient you care most about on first.

Full evening order for reference: oil cleanser, water-based cleanser, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, moisturiser, optional facial oil. Morning: cleanser, toner, essence or serum, moisturiser, sunscreen.

### The minimal three-step version

For anyone who wants the least possible, and this is a perfectly complete routine on its own:
1. **Morning:** gentle cleanse (or water rinse), moisturise, sunscreen.
2. **Evening:** cleanse (double cleanse if you wore sunscreen or makeup), moisturise.

That is it. The AAD's core advice is simply to cleanse, moisturise and protect from the sun, and the Tira article warns beginners against chasing long multi-step trends. Once the three steps feel automatic, a hydrating toner is the easiest fourth step, and one treatment serum the fifth. Masks and exfoliators are optional extras, two or three times a week at most (Tira).

## 2. Double cleansing

### What it is

Double cleansing means washing your face twice in a row in the evening, with two different kinds of cleanser:
1. **An oil-based cleanser first** (a cleansing oil or a solid cleansing balm). Oil dissolves oil, so this step lifts off sunscreen, makeup, your own sebum and sticky pollution particles, none of which rinse away well with water alone (Soko Glam, CeraVe).
2. **A water-based cleanser second** (a gel, foam, cream or milk). This removes sweat, water-based grime and any oily residue from step one, leaving skin clean without feeling tight (Soko Glam, CeraVe).

Cleansing wipes and micellar water on their own are a weak substitute; wipes in particular tend to smear things around rather than lift them off (Soko Glam).

### Why Korean skincare treats it as standard

Korean routines are built around two things: daily sunscreen, and layering light hydrating products. Sunscreen, especially the long-wearing kind, is designed to cling to skin, so an oil-based cleanser is the reliable way to remove it fully without scrubbing. The standard K-beauty evening order therefore starts with a cleansing balm or oil and then a water-based cleanser (Misshaus). Soko Glam presents it as a basic everyday habit rather than a special treatment. For acne-prone skin, leaving sunscreen and makeup residue on overnight is a common cause of clogged pores, which is why NICE and the NHS both say to remove makeup at the end of every day.

### When you need it

- Evenings after wearing sunscreen (which should be every day).
- Evenings after wearing makeup, especially long-wear or waterproof formulas.
- After heavy sweating, a day in city pollution, or any day skin feels greasy or gritty (CeraVe).
- The Tira article is more conservative: it says double cleansing is only needed if you wear heavy makeup or sunscreen. Both views lead to the same place for most people, because daily sunscreen is the goal.

### When you don't

- Mornings. Nothing has been put on your face overnight, so a single gentle cleanse, or just lukewarm water for dry or sensitive skin, is enough.
- Evenings when you wore nothing on your skin at all (no sunscreen, no makeup), for example a day indoors.
- Dry, normal or sensitive skin may not need it every evening; start a couple of times a week and watch how skin responds (CeraVe).
- If you have eczema or rosacea, check with a GP or dermatologist before adding any new cleansing step (CeraVe).

### How to do it

1. Start with dry hands and a dry face. Massage a pump of cleansing oil, or a pearl-sized scoop of balm, over the whole face in gentle circles for about 30 to 60 seconds. Spend extra time on the hairline, nose and jaw where sunscreen builds up. Avoid the eyes unless the product says it is eye-safe.
2. Wet your hands and keep massaging. A good cleansing oil or balm will turn milky when it meets water (this is called emulsifying) and that is what lets it rinse off cleanly.
3. Rinse with lukewarm water.
4. Follow with your water-based cleanser, rinse, and pat dry with a clean towel.
5. Carry on with toner and the rest of your evening routine (CeraVe, Soko Glam).

### Common mistakes

- Using a plain kitchen or carrier oil (olive, coconut). These do not emulsify, so they sit on the skin and can clog pores. Use a product designed to rinse off (Soko Glam).
- Skipping the second cleanse because the oil "felt like it did the job" (Soko Glam).
- Double cleansing in the morning as well, which over-cleanses and dries skin out.
- Using a harsh, high-foaming or soap-based second cleanser that leaves skin squeaky and tight. Pick one suited to your skin type (Soko Glam).
- Hot water and scrubbing. Warm water and fingertips only (AAD).
- Rushing step two: cleansing oil left behind can itself cause congestion.

### A note on oil cleansers (including heartleaf cleansing oils)

Cleansing oils are sold by their star botanical, and heartleaf (Houttuynia cordata) is a popular one in K-beauty for oily, acne-prone and easily reddened skin because it is marketed as soothing and lightly oil-controlling (Beauty Bay). Heartleaf cleansing oils, rice-based cleansing oils, green tea balms and so on are all the same category of product: an emulsifying oil or balm. The base does the cleaning; the botanical is a bonus. Choose by how it rinses and how your skin feels afterwards, not by the headline plant.

### By skin type (CeraVe, Soko Glam)

- Dry or sensitive: a fragrance-free, alcohol-free, creamy or milky second cleanser.
- Oily or blemish-prone: a lightweight oil or balm labelled non-comedogenic, then a gentle gel.
- Combination or normal: experiment; a balm or micellar water followed by a hydrating gel works for most.

## 3. Skin types explained

### The five types

These definitions follow the AAD categories as summarised by Cleveland Clinic:

- **Oily:** skin makes more sebum (natural oil) than it needs. Signs: shine within an hour or two of washing, visible pores, blackheads and spots, makeup sliding off.
- **Dry:** skin makes too little sebum. Signs: tightness after washing, dullness, flaking or rough patches, fine lines that show more easily, sometimes itching.
- **Combination:** an oily T-zone (forehead, nose, chin) with normal or dry cheeks and eye area. The reverse pattern is also possible. This is the most common type.
- **Normal:** neither noticeably oily nor dry, few breakouts, rarely reacts. A comfortable middle.
- **Sensitive:** defined by reactivity rather than oil level. Skin that stings, burns, itches or flushes easily, often linked to eczema, rosacea or contact dermatitis. You can be sensitive and oily, sensitive and dry, or sensitive and combination.

### How to tell at home: the bare-face test

1. Wash your face with a gentle cleanser and pat dry.
2. Put nothing on it. No toner, no moisturiser.
3. Wait 30 to 60 minutes and look in a mirror in natural light.
4. Read the result:
   - Shine across the whole face: oily.
   - Tight, dull or flaky: dry.
   - Shiny forehead and nose, comfortable or tight cheeks: combination.
   - Comfortable everywhere, no shine or tightness: normal.
   - Stinging, redness, heat or itching at any point, including during washing: sensitive (on top of whatever the oil pattern shows).

A blotting-paper version works too: press a sheet of blotting paper or a thin tissue to the forehead, nose and cheeks after the wait and see where oil shows up (Cleveland Clinic).

### Dry versus dehydrated

These are not the same thing, and mixing them up leads to the wrong products.

- **Dry skin lacks oil.** It is a skin type, often inherited and often lifelong. It wants lipids: ceramides, richer creams, gentle non-stripping cleansers, and moisturiser straight after washing (Schweiger Dermatology).
- **Dehydrated skin lacks water.** It is a temporary condition that any skin type can have, including oily skin. Signs: a dull or tired look, fine lines that soften after moisturising, tightness even though the skin is shiny, makeup settling into lines, and skin that creases rather than bouncing back when gently pinched (Schweiger Dermatology). It wants humectants (glycerin, hyaluronic acid) on damp skin, a moisturiser on top to stop the water escaping, and less exfoliating (The INKEY List, Schweiger Dermatology).
- **Oily skin can be dehydrated.** If skin is shiny but feels tight, it may be producing extra oil to compensate for lost water. Stripping it harder makes this worse; adding water and a light moisturiser helps.

### Skin type changes

Skin type is not fixed. It shifts with:
- **Age.** Skin thins and loses natural ceramides and elasticity over time, so many oily twenty-somethings become dry or combination later on (Cleveland Clinic ceramides, skin types).
- **Seasons and weather.** Hot, humid summers push skin oilier; cold air and central heating in a UK winter pull water out and leave it drier (Cleveland Clinic).
- **Hormones.** Puberty, menstrual cycles, pregnancy, breastfeeding and menopause all change oil production. The NHS lists hormone changes as a main trigger for acne (NHS acne, Cleveland Clinic).
- **Stress, diet, sleep and some medications** (Cleveland Clinic).

Practical rule for the site: repeat the bare-face test at the start of each season and after any big life change, and let the routine shift with the result.

## 4. Ingredient glossary

Each entry follows the same shape: what it is, what it does, who it suits, who should be careful or avoid it, how to introduce it, what it pairs with, and what not to combine it with. "Patch test" means the method in section 8: a small amount on the inner forearm, twice a day, for several days, watching for redness, itching or swelling.

A general rule that applies to every active below: introduce one new product at a time, give it two weeks before adding anything else, wear SPF every morning, and stop if there is persistent stinging, burning or a rash.

### Snail mucin (snail secretion filtrate)

- **What it is:** the filtered slime snails produce, collected and purified for skincare. Not vegan.
- **What it does:** mainly hydrates and softens. It contains natural humectants (including hyaluronic acid), a little glycolic acid, allantoin (a soothing ingredient) and proteins. Claims about collagen and scar healing come from small or animal studies, so treat those as possible rather than proven (Healthline). The Tira article recommends it for dry or mature skin.
- **Suits:** dry, dehydrated, dull or mature skin; most oily skin tolerates it because it is watery.
- **Be careful:** anyone with a history of allergies, as rare reactions include rash, hives and swelling (Healthline). People with mollusc or shellfish allergy should ask a pharmacist first, since the data is thin. Vegans will want to skip it.
- **Introduce:** patch test, then use daily after toner. Give it several weeks.
- **Pairs with:** hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, ceramides, panthenol.
- **Avoid combining:** no known clashes. It is a hydrator, so it can sit under almost anything.

### Mugwort (Artemisia)

- **What it is:** a leaf extract from the Artemisia plant, long used in Korea in food, teas and baths.
- **What it does:** calms redness and irritation, with antioxidant properties; a Korean study suggested the extract eased contact dermatitis symptoms (Round Lab, brand content). The Tira article describes it as soothing, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial.
- **Suits:** sensitive, red, itchy, acne-prone or mildly eczema-prone skin.
- **Be careful:** Artemisia belongs to the daisy and ragweed plant family, so anyone with ragweed, chamomile or daisy allergies should patch test carefully. Fermented mugwort "healing" claims are early-stage research.
- **Introduce:** as a toner, essence or cream after cleansing, once daily, then twice.
- **Pairs with:** centella, panthenol, ceramides, hyaluronic acid.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing specific; it is often used to soften the sting of stronger actives.

### Rice extract (rice water, rice bran, fermented rice)

- **What it is:** extracts of rice grain or bran. Fermented versions are common in Korean toners and essences.
- **What it does:** gentle brightening and evening of skin tone, plus hydration and soothing. It contains ferulic acid (an antioxidant), allantoin, and phytic acid, a very mild exfoliant that can slow pigment production (Mirai Skin, retailer content; Tira).
- **Suits:** dull, uneven or lightly marked skin that cannot tolerate stronger brighteners; dry and combination skin.
- **Be careful:** very few concerns; check for fragrance in the formula. Results are slow, roughly four to eight weeks for post-spot marks (Mirai Skin).
- **Introduce:** as an early layer (toner or essence) morning and evening.
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, vitamin C, hyaluronic acid.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Green tea (Camellia sinensis)

- **What it is:** leaf extract rich in polyphenols, especially EGCG, plus a little caffeine.
- **What it does:** antioxidant and calming; EGCG may reduce sebum and has antimicrobial activity, which is why it appears in products for oily and spot-prone skin. The evidence is mostly small or older studies (Healthline). The Tira article lists it for sensitive or oily skin.
- **Suits:** oily, combination, spot-prone and red-prone skin.
- **Be careful:** allergy is rare. Do not put used tea bags on the eyes; a cool compress does the same job with less risk (Healthline).
- **Introduce:** toners, serums or gel moisturisers, daily.
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, zinc, hyaluronic acid, salicylic acid.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Centella asiatica (cica, tiger grass, gotu kola)

- **What it is:** a plant used for centuries in Ayurvedic and Chinese medicine. Its active parts are asiaticoside, asiatic acid, madecassic acid and madecassoside (Cleveland Clinic).
- **What it does:** soothes redness and irritation, supports the barrier and wound recovery; claims about collagen and scars are early research (Cleveland Clinic). Tira lists it for redness-prone, sensitive or acne-prone skin and post-procedure care.
- **Suits:** sensitive, reactive, dry or damaged skin, and anyone whose skin is cross with them after over-exfoliating.
- **Be careful:** rare allergy (burning or contact dermatitis), so patch test on the inner arm. Cleveland Clinic suggests checking with a doctor before use if under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, or with a history of liver problems or skin cancer, though those cautions mostly stem from oral use.
- **Introduce:** leave-on creams and serums work better than rinse-off formats.
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, panthenol, ceramides; often formulated next to stronger actives to offset irritation.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Ginseng (Panax ginseng, red ginseng)

- **What it is:** root extract, often steamed and dried (red ginseng). The active compounds are ginsenosides.
- **What it does:** antioxidant; marketed for firmness, glow and brightness. Retailer content claims support for collagen and a slowdown in pigment production, with visible radiance in about four to six weeks (Mirai Skin, retailer content). The Tira article mentions circulation, brightening and elasticity.
- **Suits:** dull, tired-looking, dry or mature skin.
- **Be careful:** topical ginseng has no known contraindications and is considered suitable for sensitive skin, but rare reactions (swelling, rash, peeling) happen, so patch test on the elbow crease or behind the ear for 24 hours. Pregnancy cautions apply to swallowing ginseng, not skincare (Typology).
- **Introduce:** serum or essence after toner, once or twice daily.
- **Pairs with:** hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, peptides, ceramide moisturisers.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known; no sun-sensitivity concern.

### Heartleaf (Houttuynia cordata)

- **What it is:** a flowering plant from East and Southeast Asia, now one of the most common botanicals in Korean toners, pads and cleansing oils.
- **What it does:** soothes redness, helps regulate oil, has antibacterial properties and light hydration. Brightening and "anti-pollution" claims are retailer marketing (Beauty Bay, retailer content).
- **Suits:** oily, acne-prone and sensitive skin; good for teenagers and anyone whose skin is both spotty and easily irritated.
- **Be careful:** no documented cautions beyond patch testing; check for added fragrance or alcohol in pad formulas.
- **Introduce:** toner or pads after cleansing, once daily, building to twice.
- **Pairs with:** centella, niacinamide, low-strength salicylic acid.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Propolis

- **What it is:** a resin bees make from plant sap to seal their hives.
- **What it does:** antibacterial, antioxidant and soothing; used in ampoules and toners for blemish-prone skin (Jane Yoo MD).
- **Suits:** spot-prone, combination or normal skin wanting an antioxidant layer; sensitive skin with a patch test.
- **Be careful or avoid:** anyone allergic to bee stings, honey or beeswax should avoid it or patch test the inner forearm for several days first; reactions range from mild irritation to significant contact allergy. Not vegan. It is a supporting ingredient, not a replacement for proven acne treatments (Jane Yoo MD).
- **Introduce:** after patch testing, as a serum or toner once daily.
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, centella.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Niacinamide (vitamin B3)

- **What it is:** a form of vitamin B3, one of the most studied cosmetic ingredients.
- **What it does:** strengthens the barrier, calms redness, helps regulate oil, softens the look of pores and dark marks, and has antioxidant properties (Cleveland Clinic). It cannot physically shrink pores; it makes them less noticeable by smoothing texture and reducing oil.
- **Suits:** nearly everyone, including oily, acne-prone, sensitive, rosacea-prone and eczema-prone skin.
- **Be careful:** side effects are rare (itching, mild burning, redness). Sensitive skin should start with a lower percentage; most products contain 5% or less, and very high strengths offer no extra benefit and more risk of flushing (Cleveland Clinic). Considered a safe choice in pregnancy (Jane Yoo MD).
- **Introduce:** patch test, then once daily, then twice.
- **Pairs with:** hyaluronic acid, ceramides, vitamin C, retinol, azelaic acid, exfoliating acids. The old warning about niacinamide cancelling vitamin C is based on decades-old research on raw ingredients and does not apply to modern formulas (John Bell & Croyden).
- **Avoid combining:** nothing specific; the only real risk is piling up too many products at once (Cleveland Clinic).

### Hyaluronic acid (HA)

- **What it is:** a sugar-like molecule found naturally in skin that binds water (Harvard Health). Despite the name it is a hydrator, not an exfoliating acid.
- **What it does:** draws water into the outer skin and plumps fine lines temporarily. It does not moisturise on its own; it needs a moisturiser over the top (The INKEY List).
- **Suits:** all skin types, including oily and spot-prone, since it is non-comedogenic.
- **Be careful:** applied to dry skin in a dry, heated room, a humectant can pull water up from deeper skin and leave it feeling tighter. Signs you are using it wrong: tightness, dullness or stickiness after use (The INKEY List).
- **Introduce:** two to three drops on skin that is still damp from cleansing, within about a minute, then moisturiser within another minute or so to seal it (The INKEY List).
- **Pairs with:** glycerin, ceramides, niacinamide, vitamin C, retinol, snail mucin. Apply HA first, then the active.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing; just let leave-on acids dry before layering it (The INKEY List).

### Ceramides

- **What they are:** fats that make up roughly half of the outer skin layer; the "grout" between skin cells (Cleveland Clinic). Products use lab-made versions, labelled ceramide NP, AP, EOP and so on.
- **What they do:** rebuild the barrier so skin holds water and keeps irritants out.
- **Suits:** everyone, and especially dry, dehydrated, eczema-prone, mature or over-exfoliated skin. Natural ceramide levels fall with age and in eczema (Cleveland Clinic).
- **Be careful:** no meaningful cautions.
- **Introduce:** in a moisturiser applied to damp skin within about three minutes of washing (Cleveland Clinic).
- **Pairs with:** glycerin, hyaluronic acid, panthenol, squalane, niacinamide, petrolatum or dimethicone for very dry skin.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing. Ceramide creams are the best friend of anyone using retinoids or acids.

### Panthenol (pro-vitamin B5)

- **What it is:** a derivative of vitamin B5, usually the D-panthenol form (Paula's Choice).
- **What it does:** humectant, reduces water loss through the barrier, soothes stressed skin and may reduce sensitivity-related redness.
- **Suits:** every skin type, especially dry, dehydrated or irritated skin.
- **Be careful:** long safety record, considered safe up to around 5% in leave-on products, and not known to clog pores (though heavy cream bases might). Not known to be a concern in pregnancy; check with a midwife or GP as usual (Paula's Choice).
- **Introduce:** leave-on products (serum, moisturiser, toner) give the most benefit; daily use is fine.
- **Pairs with:** hyaluronic acid, ceramides, centella; it helps skin tolerate vitamin C and retinol.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Glycerin

- **What it is:** the most common humectant in skincare, plant-derived, listed simply as "glycerin" (The INKEY List).
- **What it does:** pulls water into the outer skin and holds it there; supports the barrier and soothes reactive skin.
- **Suits:** all skin types, including oily and acne-prone (non-comedogenic), sensitive and dehydrated.
- **Be careful:** pure glycerin on very dry skin in very dry air can pull water from deeper layers; use it inside formulated products rather than neat (The INKEY List).
- **Introduce:** already in most toners and moisturisers; apply to damp skin.
- **Pairs with:** hyaluronic acid, ceramides, niacinamide; it offsets dryness from retinol, acids and vitamin C.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Squalane

- **What it is:** a stable, plant-derived version of squalene, an oil that skin makes naturally (CeraVe).
- **What it does:** an emollient that softens, smooths and reduces water loss without a greasy feel. Non-comedogenic.
- **Suits:** dry, sensitive, eczema-prone and mature skin; light formulas also work for oily and acne-prone skin.
- **Be careful:** very few concerns; a tiny number of people find any oil texture too heavy.
- **Introduce:** a few drops as the last evening step, or mixed into moisturiser.
- **Pairs with:** ceramides, hyaluronic acid, retinol (it buffers irritation).
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Salicylic acid (BHA)

- **What it is:** a beta hydroxy acid, oil-soluble, originally from willow bark (The INKEY List).
- **What it does:** dissolves into the oil inside pores to clear blackheads, whiteheads and congestion; reduces excess oil. The NHS lists it as an over-the-counter option for acne, and BAD notes it helps mild acne.
- **Suits:** oily, congested and blemish-prone skin.
- **Be careful or avoid:** avoid while on isotretinoin (NHS). In pregnancy, high-strength peels and oral salicylates are off the table; low-strength over-the-counter washes and spot treatments are generally considered acceptable, but ask a GP or midwife first (Jane Yoo MD). Dry, sensitive or barrier-damaged skin should start with a rinse-off cleanser or skip it. Use 2% or less (Skincare by Dr V).
- **Introduce:** a rinse-off salicylic cleanser is the gentlest start; a leave-on toner or serum two to three evenings a week, built up slowly (The INKEY List). Patch test for 24 to 48 hours.
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, green tea, zinc, a ceramide moisturiser.
- **Avoid combining:** another leave-on acid in the same evening; a retinoid in the same routine when you are new to both (alternate nights instead); benzoyl peroxide on the same area at the same time unless a pharmacist or prescriber set it up. Stop both acids if redness, tightness or stinging persists (The INKEY List).

### AHAs: glycolic, lactic and mandelic acid

- **What they are:** alpha hydroxy acids, water-soluble exfoliants that loosen dead cells on the surface. Glycolic has the smallest molecule and goes deepest; lactic is a step larger; mandelic (from bitter almonds) is one of the largest, so it works more slowly and gently (The INKEY List, Phyto-C).
- **What they do:** smooth texture, brighten dull skin, soften fine lines and help fade marks. Most people notice change around six weeks (The INKEY List). Lactic acid also turns up in moisturisers for dry skin at low levels (AAD).
- **Suits:** glycolic for resilient, dull or rough skin; lactic for drier skin; mandelic for sensitive, acne-prone and darker skin tones, where its slower action lowers the risk of post-inflammatory dark marks (Phyto-C, Skincare by Dr V).
- **Be careful or avoid:** darker skin tones and sensitive skin should avoid starting with glycolic (Skincare by Dr V). All AHAs increase sun sensitivity, so daily SPF is compulsory. Pregnancy safety is not well studied; ask a GP or midwife (Phyto-C). Avoid with eczema, rosacea or a damaged barrier.
- **Introduce:** one or two evenings a week, building to three or four at most; glycolic at 5% or less to start, mandelic at 10% or less, lactic at 7% or less (Skincare by Dr V, The INKEY List). Patch test first.
- **Pairs with:** hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, ceramides, panthenol.
- **Avoid combining:** a second leave-on acid or a PHA the same night; retinoids the same night until skin is used to both (alternate instead); vitamin C in the same step. Stop if persistent redness or stinging.

### PHAs (gluconolactone, lactobionic acid)

- **What they are:** polyhydroxy acids, the gentlest family of chemical exfoliants. Their molecules are larger than AHAs, so they stay on the surface and also attract water (Dermatica, Medik8).
- **What they do:** mild smoothing and brightening with hydration and antioxidant support.
- **Suits:** sensitive, reactive, rosacea-prone, eczema-prone, dry or mature skin, complete beginners to acids, and anyone whose barrier is recovering (Dermatica).
- **Be careful:** less likely to sting or peel, but over-use still damages the barrier. SPF every morning (Dermatica).
- **Introduce:** rinse-off formats for very sensitive skin; leave-on products typically 2% to 10%; once or twice a week to start, in the evening after cleansing (Dermatica).
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, ceramides, panthenol.
- **Avoid combining:** retinoids, AHAs or BHAs in the same routine; use them at different times of day or on different days (Dermatica).

### Retinol and retinal (retinaldehyde)

- **What they are:** over-the-counter members of the retinoid family, all derived from vitamin A. Skin has to convert them into retinoic acid to use them: retinol takes two steps, retinal takes one, so retinal is stronger and faster at the same percentage (Paula's Choice). Prescription retinoids (tretinoin, adapalene, isotretinoin) are covered under adapalene below.
- **What they do:** speed up skin cell turnover and boost collagen, which improves tone, texture and fine lines, and helps keep pores clear in acne (AAD).
- **Suits:** adults with acne, uneven texture, early signs of ageing or dark marks.
- **Be careful or avoid:** not in pregnancy or when trying to conceive, and avoid while breastfeeding unless a doctor agrees (AAD, UKTIS). Not for under-16s without a doctor's say-so. People with significant redness, eczema, rosacea or very dry skin may not be good candidates (AAD). Darker skin tones have a higher risk of dark marks from irritation, so go slower (AAD). Expect dryness, peeling and sometimes a temporary flare of spots in the first weeks (BAD).
- **Introduce:** the lowest strength you can find, a pea-sized amount, two or three nights a week at first, then every other night, building up over weeks (AAD, Paula's Choice). Apply to dry skin; if it irritates, wait 30 minutes after washing (AAD pores page). Buffer with moisturiser before and after (the "sandwich") if skin is sensitive. Night only. Results take 8 to 12 weeks of regular use (Paula's Choice).
- **Pairs with:** ceramides, hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, panthenol, squalane; vitamin C in the morning.
- **Avoid combining:** retinol and retinal together; a strong exfoliating acid in the same evening while you are new to either; benzoyl peroxide applied at the same time (UK product leaflets advise against stacking other retinoid or peroxide products). Always SPF the next morning.

### Vitamin C (L-ascorbic acid and derivatives)

- **What it is:** pure vitamin C (L-ascorbic acid) is the most studied form but is unstable. Derivatives such as sodium ascorbyl phosphate, ascorbyl glucoside and tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate are more stable and gentler but work more slowly (John Bell & Croyden).
- **What it does:** an antioxidant that brightens dull skin, evens tone, fades dark marks and supports sunscreen by mopping up the damage UV causes.
- **Suits:** most adults wanting brightness or help with marks; considered a safe choice in pregnancy (Jane Yoo MD).
- **Be careful:** pure vitamin C sits at a low pH and can tingle; a brief tingle is normal, persistent stinging is a stop sign. Sensitive skin should start with a derivative. A serum that has turned deep orange or brown has oxidised and is no longer doing much (John Bell & Croyden). Some forms can trigger purging (Banner Health).
- **Introduce:** in the morning after cleansing, before moisturiser and sunscreen. For L-ascorbic acid, 10% to 20% is the useful range, with about 15% a sensible middle; buy small, opaque, airless bottles and keep them away from light and heat (John Bell & Croyden).
- **Pairs with:** vitamin E and ferulic acid (often formulated together for stability), niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, sunscreen.
- **Avoid combining:** exfoliating acids in the same step; retinoids at the same time when you are new (vitamin C in the morning, retinoid at night).

### Alpha arbutin

- **What it is:** a plant-derived relative of hydroquinone from bearberry, released slowly in skin (City Skin Clinic).
- **What it does:** slows the enzyme that makes pigment, so existing dark spots fade and new ones form less easily. Gentle, at 1% to 2% in most products.
- **Suits:** most skin types including sensitive, and all skin tones, for post-spot marks and sun spots.
- **Be careful or avoid:** avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding because safety data is limited; take care with eczema, psoriasis or rosacea; it may increase UV sensitivity, so SPF is essential. It is a mild brightener and may not be enough for deep or extensive pigmentation (City Skin Clinic).
- **Introduce:** patch test; apply before moisturiser, morning after any acid, or evening after any retinoid.
- **Pairs with:** vitamin C, niacinamide, sunscreen.
- **Avoid combining:** several strong brighteners or exfoliating acids at once without advice.

### Tranexamic acid (TXA)

- **What it is:** a molecule derived from the amino acid lysine; taken as a tablet for heavy bleeding, and used on skin for pigmentation (Dermatica).
- **What it does:** reduces excess pigment and has a calming effect, so it is used for melasma (hormonal pigmentation), post-spot marks and uneven tone.
- **Suits:** almost all skin types, including sensitive skin, and people who cannot use hydroquinone.
- **Be careful:** side effects are rare; stop and seek advice if there is swelling, redness or stinging. Very reactive skin should patch test. If you are pregnant, planning to be, or have a history of blood clots, ask a GP before using it, because the oral form carries cautions. Avoid stacking it with lots of other actives.
- **Introduce:** once daily, in the morning if you use a retinoid at night; add other actives gradually at a different time of day. Expect a visible difference at around 8 to 12 weeks (Dermatica).
- **Pairs with:** sunscreen (essential), niacinamide, vitamin C, azelaic acid.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing specific beyond "not everything at once".

### Azelaic acid

- **What it is:** a naturally occurring acid found in grains; not a hydroxy acid, so it does not exfoliate in the same way (Banner Health).
- **What it does:** fights acne bacteria, calms redness, and slows pigment production. Used for mild to moderate acne, rosacea and melasma. It is part of NICE's first-line acne options and available over the counter in the UK (NICE, BAD).
- **Suits:** most skin types including sensitive and rosacea-prone skin; one of the few acne treatments considered safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding (Banner Health).
- **Be careful:** up to one in ten people get itching, burning or scaling that usually settles within about four weeks. Over-the-counter strengths are around 10% to 12%; prescription versions are 15% and above (Banner Health).
- **Introduce:** a pea-sized amount once daily on clean, dry skin for one to two weeks, then twice daily if comfortable, followed by moisturiser and SPF. Acne improves in one to two months with full effect at about four months (Banner Health).
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, vitamin C, sunscreen.
- **Avoid combining:** stacking it with exfoliating acids, benzoyl peroxide or retinoids at the same time can be drying and irritating; use with care or on alternate days (Banner Health).

### Peptides

- **What they are:** short chains of amino acids that act as messengers to skin cells (NewYork-Presbyterian).
- **What they do:** some types signal collagen production, some carry minerals like copper, some soften expression lines, some slow collagen breakdown. Evidence is limited but growing; a few trials show better elasticity and fewer wrinkles.
- **Suits:** adults, generally thirties and up, interested in firmness and fine lines. Not useful for acne.
- **Be careful:** generally very safe; main risk is irritation or rash. A dermatologist in the source is wary of them on eczema-prone skin. Teen skin does not need them.
- **Introduce:** daily in a serum or moisturiser; benefits build with consistent use.
- **Pairs with:** hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, ginseng, ceramides.
- **Avoid combining:** no proven clashes; apply after any acids have dried, and do not expect them to replace a retinoid.

### Tea tree oil

- **What it is:** an essential oil from the Australian tea tree.
- **What it does:** antimicrobial and mildly anti-inflammatory; may help mild to moderate acne, though evidence is weaker and slower than benzoyl peroxide or salicylic acid (Healthline).
- **Suits:** people who cannot tolerate benzoyl peroxide or salicylic acid and want a spot treatment.
- **Be careful or avoid:** never use it neat; dilute heavily in a carrier or buy a ready-made product, patch test the inner elbow, keep it out of eyes, and never swallow it. It can cause irritation and contact allergy, and essential oils are a poor fit for sensitive skin (Healthline, Tira).
- **Introduce:** dab a diluted or ready-made product onto individual spots after cleansing, once or twice a day.
- **Pairs with:** a plain moisturiser.
- **Avoid combining:** other irritants on the same spot (benzoyl peroxide, acids, retinoids).

### Zinc

- **What it is:** a mineral used in two main skincare forms: zinc oxide (a mineral sunscreen filter) and zinc PCA (a soothing, oil-regulating salt) (Pai Skincare, brand content). Zinc tablets are also sold in UK pharmacies for acne (NHS).
- **What it does:** zinc oxide sits on the surface and reflects UV; zinc PCA helps regulate oil, has antibacterial action and supports the barrier.
- **Suits:** oily and blemish-prone skin (zinc PCA); sensitive skin and children for sunscreen (zinc oxide; the AAD suggests mineral filters for sensitive skin).
- **Be careful:** zinc oxide sunscreens can leave a white cast on deeper skin tones; tinted versions solve this. Supplements only within recommended daily amounts.
- **Introduce:** zinc PCA in a serum or moisturiser daily; zinc oxide in sunscreen every morning.
- **Pairs with:** niacinamide, green tea, salicylic acid.
- **Avoid combining:** nothing known.

### Benzoyl peroxide (UK pharmacy option)

- **What it is:** an antiseptic cream, gel or wash sold in UK pharmacies without prescription, and in prescription combinations (NHS).
- **What it does:** kills acne bacteria and helps unblock pores. NICE includes it in every first-line acne option, including on its own when someone wants to avoid retinoids or antibiotics.
- **Suits:** anyone 12 or over with spots, blackheads or whiteheads (NHS).
- **Be careful:** start with the lowest strength; expect dryness, peeling, itching or redness that usually settles within days or by using it less often (NHS). It bleaches fabric, towels and hair. It increases sun sensitivity, so SPF is needed. Usually usable in pregnancy but check with a doctor; if breastfeeding, do not apply to the breast area (NHS). Not while on isotretinoin (NHS). If it stings badly, stop for a few days and restart less often (BAD).
- **Introduce:** wash and dry the area, apply a thin layer once a day, then twice if tolerated. It starts working within about four weeks; see a pharmacist or GP if no better after 12 weeks (NHS).
- **Pairs with:** a plain moisturiser, niacinamide, hyaluronic acid.
- **Avoid combining:** other benzoyl peroxide or retinoid products on the same area unless a prescriber combined them; drying or exfoliating cosmetics at the same time (UK product leaflet).

### Adapalene (UK prescription-only context)

- **What it is:** a topical retinoid for acne. In the UK it is prescription-only, alone or combined with benzoyl peroxide (NHS lists it among prescription treatments; the AAD notes it is over the counter in the US, which causes confusion online).
- **What it does:** reduces inflammation and oiliness and stops pores blocking, so new spots form less often (NHS). NICE recommends adapalene with benzoyl peroxide as a first-line 12-week course for acne of any severity.
- **Suits:** anyone 12 or over whose GP decides it is appropriate.
- **Be careful or avoid:** not in pregnancy or when planning pregnancy (NICE, UK leaflet, UKTIS); ask a doctor if breastfeeding and keep it off the chest. Not on cuts, sunburn or eczema. Irritation, redness and scaling are common in the first weeks and usually ease. Avoid heavy sun and sunbeds (UK leaflet).
- **Introduce:** a thin film once daily at bedtime on clean, dry skin, a pea-sized amount per area of the face. Spots improve only after several weeks; if no change after four to eight weeks, talk to the prescriber (UK leaflet).
- **Pairs with:** a gentle cleanser, ceramide moisturiser, SPF.
- **Avoid combining:** other retinoids or peroxide products, exfoliating acids, drying toners or scrubs (UK leaflet). GlowGuide should point users to their GP rather than any shop for this one.

### SPF filters in plain terms

- **Mineral (inorganic, physical) filters:** zinc oxide and titanium dioxide. They sit on the skin and mostly reflect UV, like tiny mirrors. Good for very sensitive skin and children; more likely to leave a white cast (BAD fact sheet, AAD).
- **Chemical (organic) filters:** ingredients that absorb UV and release it as a little heat, like sponges. "Organic" here is a chemistry word, nothing to do with organic farming (BAD fact sheet). Modern chemical sunscreens are now considered less irritating than older ones, so the old "mineral is always gentler" rule is out of date (British Skin Foundation). Newer European and Korean filters (brand names like Tinosorb and Uvinul) give strong, stable UVA protection and feel lighter (Jane Yoo MD).
- **SPF:** measures protection from UVB, the burning rays. Use 30 or above (NHS).
- **UVA labels used in the UK:**
  - The letters UVA inside a circle: the product meets the EU standard, meaning its UVA protection is at least one third of its SPF (NHS, BAD fact sheet).
  - The star rating (0 to 5 stars, started by Boots): UVA protection relative to UVB. Look for 4 or 5 stars alongside SPF 30 or more; a high star count on a low SPF is still weak overall (NHS, BAD fact sheet, Cancer Research UK).
  - PA+ to PA++++: the Japanese and Korean UVA system, based on a lab test called PPD. PA++++ is the highest tier and means strong UVA protection; you will see it on Korean sunscreens sold in the UK (Soko Glam, Peach & Lily, British Skin Foundation). Two PA++++ products can still differ, because it is a category rather than an exact number (Jane Yoo MD).
- **Broad-spectrum:** protects against both UVA and UVB (NHS).

## 5. Purging versus breakout versus irritation

Starting a new active and then watching spots appear is the moment most beginners give up. This is how to read what is happening.

### Purging

- **What it is:** ingredients that speed up skin cell turnover (retinoids, AHAs, BHAs, some forms of vitamin C, and prescription acne treatments) push blocked pores that were already forming under the surface up and out sooner than they would have appeared anyway (Banner Health). BAD notes that topical retinoids can cause a temporary flare of acne for a few weeks, and isotretinoin can make things briefly worse before they improve.
- **Where:** in the places you already get spots (Banner Health, Tira).
- **What it looks like:** small spots that come to a head quickly and heal fast; often several at once (Banner Health).
- **How long:** usually settles within about four to six weeks, which is roughly one skin renewal cycle (Banner Health, Tira).
- **What to do:** keep going but go gently: use the active once or twice a week, add calming and hydrating products (centella, ceramides, panthenol, hyaluronic acid), do not pick, and wear SPF (Tira, Banner Health). Over-exfoliating on top of purging makes it worse (Tira).

### A true breakout (the product does not suit you)

- **Where:** new spots in places you do not usually break out, or spreading areas (Banner Health, Tira).
- **What it looks like:** a mix of blackheads, whiteheads and deeper, slower-healing lumps; often the jawline pattern of hormonal acne (Banner Health).
- **How long:** keeps going or gets worse past six weeks (Banner Health).
- **Common causes:** a heavy or pore-clogging moisturiser, oil or sunscreen (NICE and the NHS both say to pick non-comedogenic products), a hydrating ingredient that is not a turnover-boosting active (these should not purge), or simply too many new products at once.
- **What to do:** stop the most recent addition, go back to the basic three steps for two weeks, then reintroduce one product at a time.

### Irritation (the product is damaging your barrier)

- **What it looks like:** burning, stinging, heat, itching, redness (which can look darker or purplish on brown and black skin rather than red), tightness, flaking, small rough bumps, or a rash. This is a reaction, not purging (Banner Health, NHS contact dermatitis).
- **How fast:** can start within minutes to days of use.
- **What to do:** stop the product straight away, rinse gently, and use only a bland cleanser, a fragrance-free moisturiser and SPF until skin is calm. A cool compress or petroleum jelly can help (AAD patch test page). If a rash is severe, spreading or not settling, see a pharmacist or GP; persistent reactions to cosmetics can be a form of contact dermatitis that needs a GP (NHS).

### When to stop a product

- Any burning, swelling, hives or intense itching: stop immediately (Banner Health).
- Spots spreading to new areas or getting worse rather than better: stop (Banner Health).
- Still breaking out past six weeks: stop and see a pharmacist or GP (Banner Health).
- Persistent redness, tightness or stinging from an acid or retinoid: stop all actives and focus on barrier repair for a couple of weeks (The INKEY List).
- Mild dryness, light flaking or a short-lived tingle from a retinoid or acid is expected in the first weeks and usually passes (AAD patch test page); use it less often rather than stopping entirely, and buffer with moisturiser.

## 6. Myths and red flags for beginners

**"Natural is always better or safer."** Plants can be as irritating as anything made in a lab. Lemon juice can burn and leave dark patches, especially if skin then sees sunlight; garlic and undiluted essential oils cause blistering and contact allergy (SLMD Skincare, Healthline on tea tree). Many botanical claims in skincare rest on early or small studies (Cleveland Clinic on centella). The Tira article makes the same point: natural does not automatically mean effective or gentle. Judge an ingredient on evidence and on how your own skin reacts.

**"More steps mean better skin."** Stacking products raises the chance that ingredients clash and skin gets overwhelmed (Tira, Cleveland Clinic on niacinamide). Three good steps done daily beat ten steps done badly.

**"The SPF in my makeup or moisturiser is enough."** Almost nobody applies foundation or SPF moisturiser thickly enough to reach the protection on the label, these products are less rub- and water-resistant, they rarely cover the neck and ears, and some lack UVA protection (Cleveland Clinic, BAD fact sheet, Tira). Put a proper sunscreen on first and treat SPF makeup as a bonus layer.

**"This product shrinks pores."** Pores do not open, close or shrink. They look bigger when clogged, oily, sun-damaged or when skin loses firmness, and they look smaller when kept clear and protected. Products can only make them less noticeable (AAD, Cleveland Clinic on niacinamide).

**"Drinking more water fixes dry skin."** Hydration matters for health, but unless someone is seriously dehydrated, extra glasses of water do not plump skin or fix dryness. Moisturisers and humectants do that from the outside (WebMD, with dermatologist input).

**"Expensive means better."** Price reflects marketing, packaging and brand as much as the formula. Read the ingredients list instead; ceramides, glycerin and hyaluronic acid are in plenty of inexpensive products, and fragrance (the most common unnecessary irritant) is in plenty of pricey ones (Cleveland Clinic newsroom).

**"DIY lemon, baking soda or vinegar is a cheap exfoliant."** Lemon juice is strongly acidic and can cause burns, peeling and lasting discolouration, plus a sun-triggered reaction called phytophotodermatitis. Baking soda is strongly alkaline and strips the barrier. Neither is controlled in strength the way a formulated product is (SLMD Skincare).

**"Toothpaste dries out spots."** The ingredient that once seemed to work (triclosan) has mostly been removed. What is left (baking soda, peroxide, menthol, flavourings) burns and irritates. Use a benzoyl peroxide or salicylic acid spot treatment instead (SLMD Skincare).

**"Spots mean you are dirty."** Blackheads are not caused by poor washing; the dark colour is oxidised oil and pigment, not dirt. Scrubbing harder irritates skin and makes acne worse (BAD, NHS, AAD).

**"Cutting out chocolate or dairy will clear acne."** BAD and NICE both say there is not enough evidence to recommend any specific diet for acne; a balanced diet is all that is advised.

**"You don't need sunscreen in the UK or when it's cloudy."** Most UV passes through cloud, and UV in the UK is strong enough to damage skin from mid-March to mid-October (Cancer Research UK, NHS).

**"Darker skin doesn't need SPF."** Everyone can burn and everyone can get skin cancer. Darker skin also pigments more easily after irritation, which sunscreen helps prevent (NHS, British Skin Foundation, Cancer Research UK).

**"A tan is healthy."** There is no safe way to tan; a tan is skin damage (NHS).

**"Tingling means it's working."** A brief tingle from pure vitamin C or an acid can be normal; stinging that persists means the product is too strong or does not suit you (John Bell & Croyden).

**"Vitamin C and niacinamide cancel each other out."** Based on decades-old research on raw ingredients, not modern formulas; they can be used together (John Bell & Croyden).

**"Wait five minutes between every layer."** A few seconds of patting in is enough for most products (Soko Glam quoting dermatologists).

**"Once-a-day, eight-hour sunscreens mean no reapplying."** Dermatologists are sceptical, because thin application and rubbing leave gaps; reapply anyway (BAD fact sheet).

### Red flags to teach beginners

- Promises to cure acne overnight, "detox" skin, or permanently shrink pores.
- Ingredients that are exotic or unfamiliar with no research behind them (Tira).
- Shops selling prescription-strength retinoids or hydroquinone without a prescription.
- Suspiciously cheap sunscreen from marketplace sellers; counterfeits and badly stored stock are real risks, so buy from reputable retailers and check expiry dates (Jane Yoo MD).
- Any UK content recommending products through affiliate links without a clear "Ad" label (ASA, see section 11).
- Advice to use a child's or teenager's pocket money on retinol and acids (section 8).

## 7. SPF for UK users

### The daily rule

Wear sunscreen on your face every morning, all year, whatever the weather. The NHS says to use at least SPF 30 and that sunburn can happen in the UK even on cloudy days. Cancer Research UK adds that more than 90% of UV gets through cloud, and that UK sunlight is strong enough to damage skin from mid-March to mid-October. In the darker months the UV index is usually low, but anyone using retinoids, exfoliating acids, benzoyl peroxide or isotretinoin is more sun-sensitive and should keep going (NHS benzoyl peroxide and isotretinoin pages, AAD). A good habit is to check the UV index on a weather app: at 3 or above, sun protection steps apply (NHS, Cancer Research UK).

### What to look for on the bottle

- **SPF 30 or higher.** SPF is the UVB (burning) protection number. 50+ is the highest band (NHS).
- **Broad-spectrum.** Protects against UVA as well as UVB (NHS).
- **A UK UVA label**, at least one of:
  - UVA in a circle: meets the EU standard, UVA protection at least a third of the SPF (NHS, BAD fact sheet).
  - 4 or 5 stars on the Boots star scale (NHS, Cancer Research UK).
  - PA+++ or PA++++ on Korean or Japanese sunscreens, with PA++++ the highest tier (Soko Glam, Peach & Lily, British Skin Foundation). A Korean sunscreen sold through UK retailers may show PA alongside the UVA circle; either is fine.
- **An expiry or open-jar symbol.** The open jar with a number and M tells you how many months it lasts once opened (NHS).

### How much to apply to the face

Most people use far too little, and using half the amount can cut protection by up to two thirds (BAD fact sheet). For the face:
- About a quarter to half a teaspoon for the face alone, which is the "two finger lengths" measure: squeeze a line of sunscreen along the full length of the index and middle fingers and use all of it (AAD).
- A bit more than half a teaspoon once you include the neck and ears (BAD fact sheet).
- Commonly missed spots: ears, temples, eyelids, the sides and back of the neck, the hairline, the lips (use an SPF lip balm) and, for anyone with thinning hair, the scalp (BAD fact sheet, AAD).

### When and how often

- Apply it as the last skincare step, 15 to 30 minutes before going out, then rub it in for about a minute before makeup (BAD fact sheet, Soko Glam).
- Reapply at least every two hours when outdoors, and straight after swimming, sweating or towelling, even with "water resistant" products (NHS, BAD fact sheet, AAD).
- On an ordinary indoor day a single morning application is reasonable; on a day with windows, driving or a lunchtime walk, a midday top-up is sensible (Cleveland Clinic).

### SPF under makeup

Sunscreen goes on first, over moisturiser, and gets a few minutes to settle before primer or foundation. Makeup with SPF then adds a little extra but never replaces the sunscreen layer (Cleveland Clinic, Liz Earle). To top up over makeup, a mineral powder SPF or a tinted mineral sunscreen is the least disruptive option (Cleveland Clinic).

### Darker skin tones

Melanin gives some natural protection, roughly the equivalent of a low SPF, which is nowhere near enough on its own (British Skin Foundation). Brown and black skin can burn (sunburn may feel sore or itchy rather than look red) and can develop skin cancer (NHS, Cancer Research UK). Darker skin is also more prone to melasma and post-inflammatory dark marks, and UVA and visible light make those worse, so a high-UVA sunscreen, ideally tinted with iron oxides, is a strong choice (British Skin Foundation, AAD, Jane Yoo MD).

### Why K-beauty sunscreens are popular

Korean sunscreens tend to feel like a light serum or lotion rather than a thick cream, dry down without a greasy film, sit well under makeup, and leave little or no white cast, which matters most on medium and deep skin tones. They also use newer UV filters (brand names such as Tinosorb and Uvinul) that give strong, stable UVA protection, and they label UVA protection clearly with PA++++ (Jane Yoo MD, Peach & Lily). The practical upside is that people actually wear them every day, which beats any technically superior sunscreen left in a drawer. Cautions: buy from reputable retailers rather than marketplace listings, check for alcohol if skin is dry or reactive, and check the ingredients if a product is sold under the same name in different countries, since formulas can differ (Jane Yoo MD).

### Sunscreen is one layer, not the whole plan

The NHS and Cancer Research UK both put shade (especially 11am to 3pm in the UK summer), a hat, sunglasses and clothing alongside sunscreen rather than behind it. Sunbeds are not recommended and are illegal for under-18s in the UK (NHS).

## 8. Safety rules for the recommendation logic

Each rule gives what to avoid, what to use instead, and the source. These are the rules GlowGuide's recommendation engine should encode as hard filters, with a plain-language explanation shown to the user.

### Rule 1: Pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive

- **Avoid:** all retinoids, including retinol, retinal, adapalene, tretinoin and isotretinoin. NICE says topical retinoids are contraindicated in pregnancy and when planning pregnancy; UKTIS (the UK pregnancy medicines service) says to avoid them as a precaution because oral retinoids cause birth defects and a link for skin products cannot be ruled out. The AAD says the same.
- **Avoid:** hydroquinone, and high-strength salicylic acid (peels, oral forms). Low-strength salicylic washes and spot treatments are usually considered acceptable, but only with a GP or midwife's agreement (Jane Yoo MD).
- **Avoid:** alpha arbutin, because safety data is limited (City Skin Clinic). Essential oils and anything "exotic" with no research are also best left out (Tira).
- **Caution:** benzoyl peroxide can usually be used but should be checked with a doctor; if breastfeeding, keep it off the breast (NHS). NICE allows benzoyl peroxide with clindamycin with caution in pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- **Use instead:** azelaic acid (considered safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding; Banner Health), niacinamide, vitamin C, hyaluronic acid, glycerin, ceramides, panthenol, squalane and mineral or well-formulated sunscreen (Jane Yoo MD, Paula's Choice on panthenol).
- **Always:** tell users to check any product with their GP or midwife, and that stopping an acne treatment during pregnancy is safe (UKTIS).

### Rule 2: On isotretinoin or any prescription acne treatment

- **Avoid:** adding any extra actives. The NHS says people on isotretinoin should avoid skincare containing salicylic acid or benzoyl peroxide and other acne treatments, plus chemical peels, microdermabrasion, waxing and laser hair removal. The same logic applies to anyone on prescription adapalene or tretinoin: no shop-bought retinol, acids or peroxide on top (UK adapalene leaflet).
- **Use instead:** a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser, a ceramide-rich moisturiser applied generously, a lip balm, and SPF 30 or more reapplied regularly, with no sunbeds (NHS isotretinoin page).
- **Always:** questions about the prescription itself go to the prescriber, not the site.

### Rule 3: Diagnosed eczema or rosacea

- **Avoid:** exfoliating acids, retinoids, strong vitamin C, fragrance, essential oils, alcohol-heavy toners, scrubs and hot water. The NHS advises people with rosacea to use gentle products made for sensitive skin and to avoid heat and sun; the NHS eczema page says to avoid soaps and detergents and not to use aqueous cream as a leave-on; the AAD's dry-skin advice is fragrance-free products, no alcohol or retinoids, and warm not hot water. Dermatologists in the sources are wary of peptides and most actives on eczema-prone skin (NewYork-Presbyterian) and of double cleansing without advice (CeraVe).
- **Use instead:** barrier repair: emollients at least twice a day (NHS), ceramides, glycerin, panthenol, squalane, centella, and SPF 30 daily (NHS rosacea). If any exfoliation is wanted later, a PHA is the gentlest option (Dermatica). Azelaic acid is used for rosacea under GP guidance (Banner Health).
- **Always:** route to the GP for flares, treatment and anything new; the NHS lists urgent signs for eczema (weeping, crusting, pain, fever) and rosacea (eye pain, blurred vision).

### Rule 4: Under 16

- **Avoid:** retinoids, exfoliating acids (AHA, BHA, PHA leave-ons), high-strength vitamin C and peptide "anti-ageing" products unless a doctor has recommended them. The skin barrier is still maturing around age 12, and UK surveys found nearly half of tweens using actives reported redness, itching or irritation; the British Skin Foundation has called for clear age labelling and an end to marketing strong actives to young users (Personal Care Insights, Pai Skincare).
- **Use instead:** a gentle cleanser, a light moisturiser and SPF every morning. That is the whole routine (Pai Skincare). For spots from age 12, the pharmacy option is benzoyl peroxide, which the NHS says is suitable from 12; prescription adapalene is also licensed from 12 but only via a GP (NHS, UK leaflet).
- **Always:** flag that the site cannot give personalised advice to children and that a parent or GP should be involved. Under-18s are also banned from sunbeds in the UK (NHS).

### Rule 5: Sensitive or reactive skin

- **Avoid:** fragrance, essential oils, alcohol-heavy toners, physical scrubs, glycolic acid, and starting more than one new product at a time.
- **Patch test method (AAD):** apply a coin-sized amount of the product to the inner forearm or the crook of the elbow, twice a day, for seven to ten days, leaving it on for as long as you would on the face (rinse-off products for about five minutes). If there is no redness, itching or swelling, it is reasonable to use on the face. Behind the ear is an alternative site some brands suggest, with a 24-hour check (Typology). If a reaction appears, wash it off, stop, and use a cool compress; see a GP or dermatologist if it is severe or does not settle.
- **Use instead:** short ingredient lists, hydrators (glycerin, hyaluronic acid, panthenol), barrier builders (ceramides, squalane), soothers (centella, mugwort, heartleaf, green tea), niacinamide at low strength, PHAs if exfoliation is wanted, azelaic acid for redness, and a sunscreen they find comfortable (mineral filters or modern gentle chemical filters, per AAD and the British Skin Foundation).

### Rule 6: Mixing actives

- **Do not stack** a retinoid with a strong exfoliating acid in the same routine while new to either; alternate nights instead (Paula's Choice, Phyto-C, Dermatica). PHAs also go on separate days from retinoids, AHAs and BHAs (Dermatica).
- **Do not use** two leave-on acids on the same night (The INKEY List).
- **Do not apply** benzoyl peroxide and a retinoid at the same time unless a prescriber combined them (UK leaflet).
- **Vitamin C in the morning**, retinoids at night (John Bell & Croyden, Paula's Choice).
- **Azelaic acid** alongside acids, peroxide or retinoids only with care, because together they dry and irritate (Banner Health).
- **One new product at a time, and wait about two weeks** before adding the next, so you know what caused any reaction (Tira, Dermatica). Patch test first.
- **Every active needs daily SPF** (AAD, Dermatica, The INKEY List).
- **Hydrators and barrier products** (HA, glycerin, ceramides, panthenol, niacinamide, squalane, centella) can be combined freely with each other and with any active (Cleveland Clinic, The INKEY List).

## 9. How long results take, and when to see a GP or dermatologist

### Realistic timelines

Skin renews itself roughly every four to six weeks, so almost nothing shows a true result in under a month (Banner Health).

- **Hydration and comfort:** days. Humectants and moisturisers change how skin feels almost immediately; that is the one quick win.
- **Barrier repair after over-exfoliating or irritation:** about two to four weeks of a stripped-back routine. Irritation from a new active such as azelaic acid typically settles within four weeks (Banner Health). Purging from retinoids or acids settles in four to six weeks (Banner Health).
- **Texture and glow from exfoliants:** around six weeks for glycolic acid (The INKEY List); two to four weeks for early texture change with mandelic acid (Phyto-C); four to six weeks for radiance from ginseng or rice products (retailer content).
- **Acne:** six to twelve weeks minimum. Benzoyl peroxide starts working within about four weeks and should be reviewed at 12 weeks (NHS). NICE runs every first-line acne treatment as a 12-week course before judging it. BAD says to expect at least 12 weeks before much improvement and two to four months for the full effect, and that retinoids can make spots briefly worse first. Azelaic acid improves acne in one to two months with the best result around four months (Banner Health).
- **Pigmentation and dark marks:** months. Tranexamic acid shows a visible difference at 8 to 12 weeks (Dermatica); mandelic acid pigment changes take 6 to 12 weeks (Phyto-C); rice-based brighteners take four to eight weeks for post-spot marks (retailer content). Melasma is slower still and needs daily SPF to stop it returning (Dermatica).
- **Ageing (fine lines, firmness):** months. Retinol and retinal need 8 to 12 weeks of regular use to show (Paula's Choice); firmness changes from peptides or ginseng take 8 to 12 weeks and build with consistent use (NewYork-Presbyterian, retailer content).

The site's rule of thumb: do not judge a routine, or change it, before six weeks, and give acne and pigmentation products a full 12 weeks.

### When to see a GP (NHS route)

In the UK the GP is the first stop, and the GP decides whether to refer to a dermatologist on the NHS. Patients are entitled to ask for a referral (BAD). A pharmacist can advise on over-the-counter options first (NHS, BAD).

See a GP for:
- **Acne** that has not improved after a proper try at pharmacy products, lots of spots, deep or painful spots, any scarring, signs of infection (hot, swollen skin), or acne that is affecting mood or confidence (NHS acne, BAD). NICE says to consider referral for acne that is scarring or leaving persistent pigment changes, acne causing ongoing psychological distress, and acne that has failed two courses of treatment; nodulocystic acne and uncertain diagnoses are referred, and the very rare severe form acne fulminans is referred the same day.
- **Any mole** that changes size, shape or colour, is itchy, painful, bleeding or crusty, or any new skin mark that has not gone after a few weeks (NHS moles). Suspected melanoma is referred to a specialist, ideally within two weeks.
- **Rashes** that are persistent, recurrent or severe, or where the cause is unknown or treatment is not working; the GP may refer to dermatology (NHS contact dermatitis).
- **Eczema** symptoms or treatments not helping; urgently if skin is blistered, weeping, crusted, painful, swollen, spreading fast, or comes with a fever (NHS eczema).
- **Rosacea** symptoms; urgently if there is eye pain, redness, light sensitivity, grittiness or blurred vision (NHS rosacea).
- **Sudden changes** in skin, especially after starting a new medicine (BAD).
- **Anything painful, hot, swollen or spreading**, or a reaction to a product that does not settle after stopping it (NHS, AAD).
- **Mental health impact** from any skin condition; the NHS and BAD both treat this as a reason to seek help in its own right.

### Private options

Private dermatology exists in the UK. BAD advises getting a referral letter from an NHS or private GP (though patients can book directly), talking to the NHS GP first because some treatments are NHS-only, and noting that you can return to NHS care at any time; a private clinic cannot refer you straight into an NHS clinic, your GP does that (BAD, going private). Online prescription services exist for acne and pigmentation; they still involve a prescriber and should be treated as medical care, not shopping.

## 10. Beginner mistakes

1. **Starting everything at once.** Five new products in a week means no way to tell what helped and what hurt. One new product every two weeks (Tira, Dermatica).
2. **Over-exfoliating.** Daily acids, or acids plus scrubs plus a retinoid, strip the barrier and cause the redness, flaking and breakouts people then try to fix with more exfoliating. Leave-on acids one to three times a week is plenty for most (The INKEY List, Dermatica, Medik8).
3. **Skipping sunscreen**, or using too little, or only in summer. Every active in this document makes skin more sun-sensitive, and UV drives ageing, pigmentation and skin cancer (NHS, AAD, Cancer Research UK).
4. **Hot water.** It feels good and strips the barrier. Lukewarm water, and short showers if skin is dry (AAD).
5. **Harsh scrubs and rough flannels.** Scrubbing irritates skin and worsens acne; blackheads are not dirt (AAD, NHS, BAD).
6. **Picking and squeezing.** It drives inflammation deeper and causes scars and dark marks (NHS, NICE, BAD, AAD).
7. **Changing the routine before six weeks.** Skin needs a full renewal cycle to show anything, and acne treatments need 12 weeks (Banner Health, NICE, BAD).
8. **Using "actives" without knowing what they are.** Buying a product because it is trending, without knowing whether it exfoliates, brightens or hydrates, is how people end up with a retinoid, two acids and a vitamin C in one night. Learn the category of each product before buying (Tira, Cleveland Clinic on niacinamide).
9. **Using a hydrating serum on dry skin without a moisturiser on top.** Humectants need sealing in or they can leave skin tighter (The INKEY List).
10. **Treating tingling as proof it works.** A brief tingle can be fine; stinging, heat or burning is a stop sign (John Bell & Croyden, Banner Health).
11. **Heavy, pore-clogging products on spot-prone skin.** Coconut oil, thick balms and rich creams can block pores; choose non-comedogenic products (NICE, NHS, SLMD Skincare).
12. **Fragrance everywhere.** The most common unnecessary irritant in skincare; sensitive, eczema-prone and rosacea-prone skin should avoid it (Cleveland Clinic newsroom, AAD).
13. **Using prescription-only ingredients bought online without a prescriber.** Adapalene, tretinoin and hydroquinone need medical supervision in the UK (NHS, UK leaflet).
14. **Giving up during purging.** Small, fast-healing spots in the usual places during weeks one to six of a retinoid or acid are expected; spots spreading to new places are not (Banner Health).
15. **Copying an adult routine for a child or young teen.** Cleanser, moisturiser and SPF is the whole routine under 16 unless a doctor says otherwise (Pai Skincare, Personal Care Insights).

## 11. Affiliate transparency wording and medical disclaimer

### What UK rules require for affiliate links

The ASA (Advertising Standards Authority, which enforces the CAP advertising code) and the CMA (Competition and Markets Authority, which enforces consumer protection law) jointly publish guidance on making clear that ads are ads. The key points as they apply to a website with affiliate links:

- Content that contains, or sends people to, a link or code that earns the site money when someone clicks or buys counts as advertising. This covers trackable links from programmes such as Amazon Associates and affiliate networks.
- The disclosure has to be clear and prominent and appear before the reader engages with the content, not buried in a footer or behind a "read more" click. If a whole page is built around affiliate-linked products, the whole page is an ad and needs labelling; if only part is, that part needs labelling.
- A vague general statement that some links might earn commission is not considered enough on its own.
- Acceptable labels say plainly that something is an ad: "Ad", "Advert", "Advertisement", "Advertisement feature". Labels the guidance says to avoid include "Affiliate", "Aff", "Collab", "Sponsored", "Spon", "Supported by", "In partnership with", "Gifted" and any abbreviation readers may not recognise.
- Under consumer protection law, hiding a commercial relationship can be an unfair commercial practice. The CMA and Trading Standards can enforce this. The underlying law moved from the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 to the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, and the ASA has said its guidance stays broadly correct while it updates the legal references.
- Both the site and the brand share responsibility, and any opinions expressed must be genuine.

### Suggested wording for GlowGuide (own words, adapt as needed)

Site-wide notice, near the top of any page with affiliate links and in the footer:

> Ad: this page contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, GlowGuide may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you. This helps keep the site free. Our recommendations are based on our own research and the safety rules explained on this site, and no brand pays to be included.

Inline label beside each affiliate link or product card: "Ad" or "Ad (affiliate link)". Keep the label visible without hovering, scrolling or clicking.

On the recommendations results page, before the product list: "Ad: the products below are affiliate links. Commission never changes which products we suggest or the order they appear in."

### Suggested medical disclaimer (own words, adapt as needed)

> GlowGuide is general skincare education, written for beginners. It is not medical advice, it does not diagnose any condition, and it is not a replacement for seeing a pharmacist, GP, midwife or dermatologist. Skin reacts differently from person to person. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, under 16, using any prescription skin treatment, or living with a diagnosed skin condition such as eczema, rosacea, psoriasis or acne, please check with a healthcare professional before changing your routine. Stop using any product that stings, burns, swells or causes a rash, and see a GP if symptoms do not settle. Contact a GP promptly about any mole or skin mark that is new, changing, bleeding, itchy or painful. If a reaction is severe or you have trouble breathing or swelling of the face or throat, call 999.

Where the site encodes the safety rules in section 8, show the relevant one-line version next to the recommendation (for example: "You told us you are pregnant, so we have left out retinoids, hydroquinone and strong acids. Please check any product with your GP or midwife.").
